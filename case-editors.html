Two editors, one shift

AristoTelos Workforce Management System · Web & Mobile · solo designer

Marta skipped breakfast and needs her lunch break half an hour earlier. (Or she wants to catch the soap opera reprise she missed yesterday. We suspect. We don't ask.) No biggie for a schedule but quite a big one for her manager.

Shifts are lively organisms. Small adjustments happen all day. But shifting Marta's lunch means for her manager to leave the floor and walk to a computer in the office, which makes it too expensive of a favor for Marta to ask.

That is not the product we want to build. Letting managers adjust shifts as they go makes them efficient, and the workplace more accommodating for the people in it.

So the mobile app needed a shift editor. My call was not to invent one, but extend what managers already know from the web editor.

What I found

The web editor was lacking any visual hierarchy and direct controls, despite the fact that parts of a shift were chronologically aligned on a timeline.

Shifting Marta's lunchbreak half an hour early? Very well, in Marta's shift editor select lunch break and a pop up form appears. Find the field labeled 'start' and type in the desired value and confirm.

Sorry Marta, this is too much effort, I don't have time for all that.

Any video editor has solved this a long time ago. Drag a block to move it. Pull its edge to extend or shorten it. Easy as that, this could be a no brainer done in two seconds, you are welcome Marta!

So the timeline became the editor. The form is still there, for when you need to be exact to the minute, change the type, add or remove a part.

Web editor: form vs. direct manipulation

Then I added depth

The hierarchy came out of the roster work. The relationships between the parts of a shift were in the business logic but nowhere on the screen.

  • Absence sits on top. It overrides everything underneath, so it covers everything underneath, visually.
  • Activities are the main flow. The spine of the day.
  • Breaks interrupt activities, so they cut right through them.
  • Parallel activities sit alongside. Lower priority, less space, unable to exist on their own.
  • Overtime sits at the bottom. Time that doesn't interfere with anything else, just marks the time worked extra.
  • Labels get their own layer. They mark sections of a shift independently of everything else.

The visual rule enforces the business rule. The model does not have to be taught, it can be seen.

Depth wasn't only about clarity. The shift view had to stay narrow, because managers open several at once to compare them and copy parts between them. The extra information couldn't cost extra height.

Depth: the layers of a shift

How I got there

Four versions, each answering the same question differently: which parts of a shift are objects, and which are conditions.

I picked the one that really shows the rules. If a layout lets you draw a shift that can't legally exist, sooner or later someone will try to save one.

One shift can carry every layer at once: absence, activities, breaks, parallel activities, overtime and labels, all in one day. Rare, but legal. I call it a horror shift, and I designed against it on purpose: an editor that survives the worst thing the rules allow doesn't need a special handling for the regular shifts of ordinary Martas.

Same model, on a phone

The constraints here were structural, not visual. The shift and its editor have to fit one phone screen. Keep the model managers are already familiar with, god forbid, users need to learn something new. Proportional time display was already out; I established that designing the roster.

Managers read shifts as columns. The shift detail I built for the roster: each layer its own named vertical stack, parts inside it as cards in chronological order with their times written on them, empty columns hidden. See the roster case study.

It shows the model correctly but it is not interactive, yet.

The editor was driven from this view. The same move as on web, where the timeline that displayed the shift became the timeline that edits it. Managers don't learn a second layout, they learn a button and the designer doesn't have to invent one either.

Shift detail as columns

Edit mode. Select a shift, press edit button, visible only to people with the rights to use it and every shift part becomes interactive. Pressing a part opens its form in the lower half of the screen. The part stays selected and the shift stays visible above it, so you can see what you are changing and what it sits between.

Move lunch to 11:30, save. The afternoon activity stretches into the gap. One part edited, the rest adjusted automatically.

Each column keeps its own + Add new, so a part is created inside the layer it belongs to. (But don't worry if you didn't hit the right column, you can change the type in the form.)

Below the shift sit the things that belong to the shift and not to its parts: details, properties, note, tags, delete. The shift view collapses to make room for them.

This way managers can adjust shifts wherever they happen to be with their smartphones.

Where it is now

Unfortunately, no usability data on any of this, just company internal evaluation of what represents the business rules right and what is doable.

Web editor is in use, mobile app design finished and handed over. When it ships is not in my hands.
